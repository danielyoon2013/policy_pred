The plaintiff brought this action upon a written contract for the sale of cotton goods, alleging breach by the defendant in failing to accept delivery on the date stipulated. The defendant pleaded by way of answer that performance had been excused by reason of a sudden and material rise in price, contending that such fluctuation amounted to a frustration of the commercial venture contemplated by the parties. The trial court overruled the defense, holding that mere market movement, however severe, does not relieve a party of obligations freely assumed by written undertaking. Upon appeal we are asked to disturb that ruling. We decline. The contract before us is plain in its terms, and the doctrines of impossibility and frustration are reserved for circumstances genuinely beyond the parties' contemplation, of which a price change, however unwelcome, is not one. The judgment of the lower court is accordingly affirmed.

The Senate yesterday took up debate upon the proposed schedule of duties on imported textile manufactures. Senator Smith of Indiana opened the discussion with a lengthy address in which he urged the necessity of protecting domestic mills from what he characterized as the ruinous competition of foreign establishments enjoying lower wages and longer working hours. Senator Walsh of Massachusetts replied that any further increase in the schedule would inevitably provoke retaliation abroad and depress the export trade in machinery and finished cotton goods, with consequences for employment in the very districts the bill purported to protect. The discussion continued late into the afternoon and was adjourned without a vote, the chair undertaking to call the matter again at the earliest convenience. Observers report that the administration is privately of the view that the existing schedule, while not ideal, is preferable to the renewed agitation a higher one would occasion among trading partners.

The Federal Reserve Board has issued a statement explaining its recent decision to maintain the discount rate at its present level, notwithstanding the urging of certain regional banks to reduce the rate further. Chairman Eccles, in remarks to the press, observed that while the volume of credit in commercial use has shown some improvement over the past quarter, the underlying movement of business activity remains hesitant, and a further easing of credit would risk encouraging speculative enterprises rather than the productive ones whose recovery the Board most desires to foster. He expressed the view that the proper office of the Board in the present circumstances is to maintain a posture of watchful patience, neither inviting new excesses by an undue liberality of credit, nor depressing legitimate enterprise by an unwarranted stringency. The statement was received with general approval in financial quarters, though some commentators of the agricultural districts continue to argue for more aggressive action.
